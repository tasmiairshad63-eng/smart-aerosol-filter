Paper 1 — The foundation: capture physics

Zhang et al. reveal what actually happens when aerosols land on nanofibers.

Research question

How do different aerosol types behave after being captured on polyimide nanofibers at the nanoscale?

Methodology

In situ platform; polyimide nanofibers; wetting droplets, non-wetting droplets, and solid particles.

Key finding

Captured aerosols do not remain static. Droplets can move or coalesce; solid particles form dendritic deposits.

Nanoscale aerosol behavior on one fiber
Wetting droplet: small contact angle → moves/coalesces Non-wetting droplet: large contact angle → fixed shape Solid particles: fixed on fiber → dendritic growth

Paper 1 — why it matters and where it stops

Strong scientific mechanism, but limited practical functionality.

Paper 1 explains particle–fiber interaction, wetting behavior, movement, coalescence, and dendritic loading.

Why useful?

It transforms filtration from a black-box process into observable nanoscale behavior.

Design implication

Performance depends not only on pore size, but also on surface chemistry and aerosol morphology.

Gap to carry forward

After harmful aerosols are trapped, the filter still needs disinfection, regeneration, or warning capability.

NEXT QUESTION: Can a captured aerosol be deactivated?

Paper 2 — the practical leap: self-sanitizing mask

Li et al. move from passive capture to an active, reusable, biodegradable mask.

Research question

Can a contaminated mask be cleaned by light and reused while maintaining filtration and breathability?

Material system

Electrospun PVA/PEO/cellulose nanofiber mask; esterification; photocatalytic N-TiO₂/TiO₂ deposition.

Reported performance

Around 100% bacterial disinfection in 10 min; 98.7% particle filterability; 83.4 L min⁻¹ airflow.

Mechanism: light-triggered rejuvenation
TiO₂ + light → reactive oxygen species → disinfection

Paper 2 — what is solved and what remains open

It is the most practical paper, but it is still not a sensing system.

Solved by Paper 2

Self-sanitization after microbial exposure
Reusability by light irradiation
Biodegradable mask concept
High filtration and breathability balance
Strong public-health and waste-reduction relevance

Still missing

No color signal for toxic gases
No real-time warning of filter saturation
No VOC adsorption function
No integrated exposure feedback for the wearer
Needs real-world aerosol diversity testing

Paper 3 — the smart filter: filtration + adsorption + sensing

Lee et al. add intelligence using nanofiber/nanonet architecture, dye sensing, and MOFs.

Research question

Can one air filter remove particles and toxic gases while giving a fast visible gas warning?

Material system

PAN nanofiber/nanonet membrane with bromocresol purple; UiO-66-NH₂ MOF-decorated mesh support.

Reported performance

High quality factor; 5 s reversible colorimetric NH₃ sensing at 20 ppm; acetaldehyde removal with negligible pressure-drop increase.

Nanofiber webPM2.5 and sub-120 nm aerosol capture
MOF meshCH₃CHO adsorption and chemical selectivity
BCP dye zoneFast, visible NH₃ color signal

Why it is future-looking

It changes the filter from a passive barrier into a responsive air-treatment device.

Proposed layered design

P1 gives capture-design logic; P2 contributes self-sanitization; P3 contributes sensing and gas removal.

Layer 1 — Biodegradable electrospun nanofiber scaffold Mechanical support + aerosol capture
Layer 2 — Photocatalytic visible-light coating Self-sanitization + regeneration
Layer 3 — MOF-loaded mesh/interlayer VOC adsorption + chemical selectivity
Layer 4 — Colorimetric dye/sensor zone User-visible warning + saturation indication

Design logic from the paper chain

P1: learn how loading and wetting evolve on nanofibers.
P2: deactivate captured biological contamination through photocatalysis.
P3: combine particle filtration, gas adsorption, and visible sensing.

Target outcome

A sustainable filter that is protective, regenerable, and informative for the user.
