Paper 1 - scientific foundation

What happens when aerosols land on nanofibers?

Research question

How do different aerosol types behave after being captured on polyimide nanofibers at the nanoscale?

Methodology

In situ investigation platform; polyimide nanofibers; comparison of wetting liquid droplets, non-wetting liquid droplets, and solid particles.

Key finding: captured aerosols do not remain static. Their evolution depends on wetting behavior and aerosol morphology.

Paper 1 - significance and limitation

Mechanistically strong - but still a passive filter

What Paper 1 explains

  • Particle-fiber interaction at the nanoscale
  • Wetting behavior, movement, and coalescence
  • Dendritic particle loading on fibers
  • Why surface chemistry matters alongside pore size
  • How filtration becomes observable rather than a black box

What remains missing

  • No disinfection after harmful aerosols are trapped
  • No regeneration or reuse strategy
  • No warning of exposure or filter saturation
  • No gas removal or sensing function
  • No direct user-facing protection response

Paper 2 - practical leap

Self-sanitizing, reusable, biodegradable mask

Li et al. move from passive capture to active surface decontamination under light.

Material system

Electrospun PVA/PEO/cellulose nanofiber mask; esterification; photocatalytic N-TiO2/TiO2 deposition.

Reported performance

About 100% bacterial disinfection in 10 min under 0.1 sun simulation/natural sunlight; 98.7% particle filterability; 83.4 L min-1 airflow.

Paper 2 - critical evaluation

It solves active protection - but the user still cannot see exposure

Solved by Paper 2

  • Self-sanitization after microbial exposure
  • Light-driven reusability
  • Biodegradable mask concept
  • High filtration and breathability balance
  • Clear public-health and waste-reduction relevance

Still missing

  • No visible signal for toxic gases
  • No real-time warning of filter saturation
  • No VOC adsorption function
  • No integrated exposure feedback for wearer
  • Needs broader real-world aerosol testing

Paper 3 - smart multifunctional filter

Filtration + adsorption + visible gas sensing

Lee et al. combine a nanofiber/nanonet membrane, a halochromic dye, and a MOF-decorated mesh.

MOF mesh CH3CHO adsorption
BCP nanofiber/nanonet NH3 color signal
Nanofiber web PM2.5 / sub-120 nm aerosol capture
Output filtered air + visible warning

Reported performance

  • High quality factor for sub-120 nm particles
  • Reversible colorimetric NH3 sensing in 5 s at 20 ppm
  • UiO-66-NH2 mesh removes acetaldehyde
  • Negligible pressure-drop increase from the MOF mesh
Bridge to research gap: Paper 3 gives an exposure signal and removes gases, but the material system is not yet self-sanitizing or biodegradable.

Aerosol nanotechnology needs a material system that is effective, active, intelligent, breathable, and environmentally responsible at the same time.

Scientific tension: adding functions can increase pressure drop, reduce breathability, lower long-term stability, or complicate manufacturing. Therefore, integration needs an optimization strategy - not only the addition of more components.

Proposed methodology

How would the idea be tested?

A credible research idea needs a material design, a challenge test, measurable outcomes, and a clear optimization criterion.

Fabricate

Electrospin biodegradable nanofiber scaffold; tune diameter, porosity, and surface chemistry.

Functionalize

Add photocatalyst + MOF/dye layer while minimizing pressure drop.

Challenge-test

Expose to aerosol sizes, PM2.5 simulants, bioaerosol surrogate, NH3, and VOC gases.

Measure

Quantify filtration efficiency, pressure drop, disinfection rate, color response, VOC removal, and stability.

Optimize

Balance protection, breathability, reusability, biodegradability, and sensing reliability.

Success criteria: high particle filtration + low pressure drop + visible sensing response + repeated light-driven disinfection + stable performance after multiple contamination-cleaning cycles.
