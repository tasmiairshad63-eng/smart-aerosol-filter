01 Visual mechanisms

What each paper actually does

Before comparing results, see the central idea. Explore each paper and the functional step it adds to aerosol filtration.

Paper 1 · Zhang et al. 2018

How captured aerosols evolve on nanofibers

Wetting droplets can move and coalesce. Non-wetting droplets remain more fixed. Solid particles accumulate as dendritic deposits on the fiber.

Capture is not static: particle–fiber interaction depends on wetting, surface chemistry, and aerosol morphology.
Paper 2 · Li et al. 2021

Light-triggered self-sanitization

An electrospun biodegradable mask carries N-TiO₂/TiO₂. Under light, photocatalysis produces reactive oxygen species that disinfect captured bacteria.

TiO₂ + light → reactive oxygen species → microbial disinfection
Paper 3 · Lee et al. 2023

One membrane: filtration, adsorption, and sensing

A PAN nanofiber/nanonet captures fine particles. UiO-66-NH₂ MOF removes acetaldehyde. Bromocresol purple gives a rapid visible ammonia warning.

Nanofiber web → PM capture   |   MOF mesh → VOC adsorption   |   dye → NH₃ signal
02 The research pathway

Every paper solves one part of the same problem

Read them as a sequence, not three unrelated articles: mechanism first, practical protection second, intelligent functionality third.

STEP 01

Understand capture

P1 makes filtration observable at the nanoscale. It shows why surface chemistry and wetting can change particle loading after capture.

STEP 02

Deactivate what is trapped

P2 responds to the hygiene problem: a filter that collects biological contamination should also be cleanable and reusable.

STEP 03

Make the filter informative

P3 adds VOC removal and a human-readable chemical warning, turning a barrier into a responsive device.

Paper 1

Capture physics on polyimide nanofibers

Mechanistic foundation · Nano Letters

What problem does it solve?

Filtration is often treated as a black box: particles arrive and are counted before and after the filter. This work directly investigates how wetting droplets, non-wetting droplets, and solid particles behave after touching a nanofiber.

What is the useful design lesson?

Filter performance is influenced by more than pore size. Fiber chemistry and the type of aerosol can determine whether droplets spread, merge, remain fixed, or form deposits.

What does it leave open?

It explains capture but does not disinfect the collected material, adsorb gases, or tell a user when the filter is risky to use.

Paper 2

A reusable self-sanitizing biodegradable mask

Practical protection · ACS Nano

What problem does it solve?

Disposable masks can accumulate microbial contamination and create waste. The paper tests whether a photocatalytic nanofiber mask can be disinfected with light while maintaining filtration and breathability.

What is the useful design lesson?

Functional coatings can give a filter a regeneration step, so the material is not simply discarded after it captures microbes.

What does it leave open?

The system does not provide real-time chemical exposure feedback, gas adsorption, or a visible warning about saturation.

Paper 3

A multi-scale MOF-functionalized smart membrane

Intelligent air treatment · Chemical Engineering Journal

What problem does it solve?

Fine particles and hazardous gases can be present at the same time. The membrane combines particulate filtration, acetaldehyde adsorption, and a rapid colorimetric ammonia signal.

What is the useful design lesson?

Different layers can be assigned different jobs: nanofibers for capture, MOFs for selective gas adsorption, and dyes for an immediate visible response.

What does it leave open?

The platform is multifunctional but does not integrate photocatalytic self-sanitization or a biodegradable substrate.

Evidence behind the pathway

Why those results matter together

Paper 2 provides a convincing route for regeneration after bioaerosol capture. Paper 3 demonstrates that a membrane can add visible sensing and VOC adsorption without losing its filtration role.

The missing combination: a sustainable scaffold that can capture particles, self-sanitize, remove gases, and communicate exposure at the same time.

05 The question developed from the gaps
Could one filter combine capture physics, self-sanitization, VOC removal, and user-visible warning in a biodegradable layered nanofiber platform?
From P1

Use nanoscale capture behavior to select fiber chemistry and morphology that manage particle loading.

From P2

Add a visible-light photocatalytic layer so captured microbial contamination can be deactivated.

From P3

Use MOFs for VOC adsorption and a colorimetric zone for fast exposure feedback.

Research gap

Join these functions without making the filter too dense, unsafe, difficult to regenerate, or non-sustainable.

07 Final verdict

Filters should not only trap aerosols. They should respond to them.

P1 explains how capture works. P2 makes capture safer through self-sanitization. P3 makes filtration smarter through sensing and gas removal. Together, they motivate a next-generation sustainable aerosol nanofilter.
